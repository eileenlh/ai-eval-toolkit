The AI Reality Check

Evaluate AI outputs for your research by deciding what deserves scrutiny, checking the evidence, and making an accountable choice.

Don’t verify everything. Allocate trust first.

AI produces fluent language by predicting likely text—not by guaranteeing meaning or truth. Treat each answer as a bundle of claims, then spend your attention where the consequences are highest.

Ask three questions before checking

1

Stakes

What happens if this claim is wrong? A cited argument or decision carries more risk than background information.

2

Verifiability

How cheaply can you check it? One authoritative source may settle it; a disputed claim may require wider searching.

3

Grounding

Did the tool retrieve and cite evidence, or generate an unsupported answer from its training?

Your judgment

High stakes + low grounding → scrutinize deeply.

Low stakes + uncontested → accept provisionally and move on.

Start here

  1. Identify the claims.
  2. Choose an effort level.
  3. Apply only the needed checks.
  4. Use, revise, reject, or investigate.

Core takeaway

Don’t ask whether to trust AI. Decide how much, on which claim, and spend your checking where it counts.

Choose the depth of checking

The deliverable is the allocation of effort—not an immediate verdict on every claim.

Claim profileEffort levelWhat to do
High stakes, low groundingDeep auditRun the full toolkit and go outside the model.
Moderate stakes, cheap to checkQuick checkMake one pass against an authority, then move on.
Low stakes, uncontestedProvisional useAccept provisionally and spend attention elsewhere.

The Layered AI Evaluation Framework (LAEF)

LAEF is a learner-facing workflow for moving from a plausible AI answer to an accountable decision. Triage determines how deeply to investigate; the three layers determine what kind of checking to do.

Why three layers? Four analytical dimensions sit underneath the framework: content quality, evidence quality, behavioral robustness, and risk or calibration. LAEF translates those dimensions into three actions learners can actually perform. 

LAEF at a glance

1

Verify source–claim fit

Is the evidence real, and does it support what the AI says?

2

Triangulate and preserve context

Does the claim remain defensible across independent evidence and the broader conversation?

3

Stress-test and judge risk

Is the answer stable, appropriately uncertain, and safe enough for the task?

How the analytical dimensions map to LAEF

1. Source VerificationEvidence quality + part of content qualityEstablish that a citation is real and determine whether it supports the generated claim.
2. Cross-ReferenceContent quality + evidence qualityTest the claim against independent evidence, compare scope and context, and preserve credible disagreement.
3. Model LimitationsBehavioral robustness + risk and calibrationStress-test the answer and identify conditions under which trust in the model should decrease.
1

Verify the source and source–claim connection

This layer separates two questions that AI answers often blur: “Is the claim true?” and “Does this particular source support it?” A real citation can still be irrelevant, misrepresented, too narrow, or taken out of context.

What to check

  • Resolve the DOI, URL, title, or author and reach the actual source.
  • Confirm that the bibliographic details match.
  • Read the abstract, methods, findings, and limitations as needed.
  • Locate the exact passage or result that could support the claim.
  • Search more than one database before labeling a citation fabricated.

Warning signs

  • The identifier resolves to a different publication.
  • The source is real, but the AI overstates or reverses its findings.
  • A review, commentary, or news story is presented as direct evidence.
  • The cited source cannot support the population, date, or causal claim given.

Layer 1 decision: Keep the citation, correct the claim, replace the source, or investigate further.

2

Triangulate the claim and preserve context

Layer 1 checks a particular source. Layer 2 asks whether the claim survives the wider evidence landscape. The goal is not to force consensus, but to distinguish convergence, boundary conditions, and credible disagreement.

What to do

  • Restate the claim precisely and separate direct support from inference.
  • Consult an independent source, preferably using a different method or evidence base.
  • Compare population, geography, period, definitions, methods, and conditions.
  • Look for systematic reviews, guidelines, or disciplinary consensus when appropriate.
  • Search for disagreement and explain it rather than averaging it away.

Authority hierarchy

  1. Independent search or original source
  2. Recent review, guideline, or consensus
  3. Subject expert or curated resource
  4. A second AI system—for comparison only

Layer 2 decision: Accept the claim with context, narrow it, qualify uncertainty, present competing interpretations, or reject it.

3

Stress-test the model and judge the risk

You do not need to know the correct answer in advance to notice unreliable model behavior. Reduce trust when reasonable changes in wording, framing, or user stance produce incompatible answers without new evidence.

Stability

  • Paraphrase the request while preserving its meaning.
  • Change formatting, order, or examples.
  • Test neutral, supportive, and skeptical frames.
  • Repeat the request and compare outputs.
  • Try another model or interface when the task warrants it.

Trustworthiness

  • Does confidence match the strength of the evidence?
  • Is a debated question presented as settled?
  • Does the answer flatter or follow the user’s preferred position?
  • Could bias, ambiguity, harmfulness, or stale training data matter?
  • Is the model retrieving current sources or relying on training-data memory?

Layer 3 decision: Use the output as a provisional aid, rerun under controlled variations, move outside the model, or stop using it for this task.

Keep an evaluation record for consequential work

For reproducible or high-stakes evaluation, document enough context for another person to understand what the system did and how you checked it.

Record the system

  • Model, version, interface, and date
  • System instructions and exact prompts
  • Browsing, retrieval, or tool status
  • Relevant sampling or generation settings

Record the evaluation

  • Repeated outputs and prompt variants
  • Comparison sources and search strategy
  • Observed uncertainty, bias, harm, or instability
  • Your final use, revision, rejection, or escalation decision

Where to verify

Use databases and tools for source verification and cross-reference checks. Most are free or library-licensed.

  • PubMedStart with biomedical literature.
  • Retraction Watch DatabaseCheck whether a paper has been retracted.
  • UlrichswebCheck whether a journal is peer-reviewed and open access.
  • Cochrane Library · MedlinePlusUse systematic reviews and NIH or federal consensus for cross-reference.

Verification reminders

  • Verify widely; one index is not proof of fabrication.
  • Check what a source actually supports.
  • Verification is access-bounded.
